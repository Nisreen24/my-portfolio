Making clinical trial recruitment easier to navigate, without compromising patient privacy.

CRITERRA is a role-based clinical trial recruitment platform connecting sponsors, healthcare organizations, doctors and eligible patients through one coordinated enrollment journey.

  • Clinical Trial Recruitment
  • Role-Based SaaS
  • Healthcare Platform

Intro

Clinical trial recruitment involves multiple stakeholders working toward the same outcome, but they should not all see the same information.

CRITERRA connects existing patient databases with relevant trials. It is not a manual patient-entry tool: the platform handles trial creation, eligibility matching, referrals, doctor review, consent and enrollment tracking, with reporting and role-based access on top.

Problem

Recruitment is a shared process, but every participant needs a different view of it. Sponsors need performance without patient identity. Organizations need fast referral tools. Doctors need clinical context to decide. Admins need oversight across everything.

Solution

I designed CRITERRA as one role-based product ecosystem built around a shared recruitment pipeline, with permissions, navigation, patient visibility and actions adapting to each role instead of four disconnected products.

Direction

One recruitment journey. Four purpose-built experiences. Matched → Referred → Doctor Review → Consent → Enrolled became the mental model every role reads, at the level of detail its responsibility requires.

Process

Design challenge

How do you design one recruitment system for four roles that should never have the same level of access?

The same patient can appear in a shared recruitment journey while being represented completely differently depending on who is viewing the system. Aggregated for a sponsor. Named for a pharmacy referring them. Clinical for the doctor reviewing them.

Every decision on this project balanced operational visibility, patient privacy, clinical access and recruitment efficiency inside one application shell.

Issue #1

The workflow was shared. The responsibilities were not.

All four roles contribute to the same five stages, but they touch them differently. The admin oversees the system. The CRO reads aggregated performance. The organization refers patients. The doctor reviews and decides.

Instead of designing separate disconnected products, I used one shared system architecture and adapted navigation, visibility and actions by role. Same topbar, same sidebar logic, same grid and design system. Different menu, content, tables, permissions and actions.

Same shell. Admin sees platform operations, trials, organizations, referrals, pipeline and reports.

Fig 1. One application shell adapting to the active role. Roles switch from the profile menu.

Issue #2

Recruitment visibility could not come at the cost of patient privacy.

CROs need to understand recruitment performance, pipeline progress, enrollment and which participating organizations contribute referrals to their trials. They must never see personally identifiable patient information.

CRO views were built around anonymized and aggregated recruitment information, scoped to the CRO’s own trials and network, while identifiable patient data remained available only where operational or clinical workflows required it. The CRO also has no organization management: no Organizations page, no Add Organization.

Issue #3

Pharmacy teams didn’t need another enterprise dashboard.

The organization workflow had one primary purpose: identify eligible patients and move them into the referral journey with as little friction as possible.

The organization experience became tablet-first, simplified and action-driven. Four menu items. Patient identification, eligibility context, referral actions and status tracking, in that order. No system settings, no heavy analytics, no AI-looking eligibility scores.

Issue #4

Complex trial criteria needed to remain understandable.

Trial creation contains complex eligibility logic, so the workflow was broken into progressive steps rather than exposing the full configuration at once. Basic information, then criteria, then details, then participating organizations, then review and publish.

The criteria builder separates inclusion from exclusion, one criterion per entry. Destructive actions on trials stay with the admin; CRO permissions remain the safer model unless a screen explicitly allows otherwise.

Issue #5

Enrollment progress needed to be readable before it was analyzable.

The pipeline became the shared mental model of the platform, letting every role understand where recruitment is progressing or dropping off before opening a report.

Five stages, one order, the same semantic colors everywhere: blue for progress, green for enrolled, amber for pending, red for declined, gray for archived.

Issue #6

The doctor experience needed depth without complexity.

Doctors can access identifiable patient information because medical review requires it. What they did not need was a system to navigate. Doctor navigation was intentionally reduced to two items: Dashboard and Patients.

Scheduling lives inside Patient Details rather than becoming a separate destination. The doctor experience was organized around the patient rather than around system modules.
