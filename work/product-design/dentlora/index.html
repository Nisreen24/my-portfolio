Turning fragmented dental care into one connected record for clinics, doctors and patients

Intro

Dentlora is a multi-country dental care platform built by SwiftX, connecting patients, doctors, clinics and administrators in one system. It covers the full care journey: discovery and booking, treatment, prescription, follow-up and ongoing care tracking.

The MVP shipped as four interconnected products at once: a patient app, a doctor workspace, a clinic dashboard and an admin dashboard, in Arabic and English, for Egypt, Canada and the USA.

Over 20 weeks, working with a product manager and the engineering team, I owned the product design end to end: user flows, information architecture, the UI system and the developer handoff. Two audiences, one record: patients and caregivers on one side, dentists and clinic staff on the other.

Problems

Dental care was split across phone calls, paper files and chat apps. Clinics confirmed payments by hand, doctors wrote notes wherever they could, and patients lost track of plans between sessions. Nothing was one record.

Solution

I designed one status vocabulary shared by clinic, doctor and patient, a treatment plan lifecycle that keeps patients attached after each session, symptom-based doctor matching, and a focused doctor workspace with autosaving notes.

Results

Four products shipped from one design system in two languages. Every appointment, plan and prescription now lives in one patient record visible to the doctor, the clinic and the patient, with country-scoped access ready to scale.

Process

Design challenge

How do you keep one care journey connected across four products, three countries and two languages?

There was no existing product to iterate on. Research framed two problems: patients drop off between finding a clinic and finishing their treatment, and dentists lose time on scheduling, records and follow-ups. Rather than design screen by screen, I started from the things every role shares:

  • One appointment status vocabulary
  • One patient record, read by different roles with different permissions
  • Country-scoped data with the same interface everywhere
  • Arabic and English from the first component, not as an afterthought

Issue #1

Booking and payment lived in different places so I designed one status everyone reads.

A patient booked in the app, the clinic confirmed payment on the phone, and the doctor found out when the patient sat down. Three people, three versions of the same appointment.

I mapped the whole lifecycle into four states the clinic, the doctor and the patient all see:

  1. New, the moment the patient confirms a booking with the fee summary
  2. Ready, when clinic staff confirm payment
  3. In Progress, when the clinic starts the session
  4. Completed, when the doctor ends it

The same chip, the same color, in every product. Nobody has to call anyone to know where an appointment stands.

Issue #2

Patients disappeared between sessions so I designed a treatment plan lifecycle that keeps them attached.

A root canal is not one visit. Without structure, the second and third sessions depended on the patient remembering to call back. Care ended when the appointment ended.

After a completed session the doctor can now create a plan made of sessions, each with a title, description and price. The patient sees the whole timeline, schedules the next session from the app, and the clinic confirms its payment the same way as any appointment.

Follow-up closes the loop: the doctor decides whether one is needed, the patient reports symptoms from home, and the answer lands in the doctor’s pre-brief before the next visit. The plan, not the calendar, holds the relationship together.

Issue #3

Patients don’t speak in specialties so I designed symptom-based matching.

Nobody searches for an endodontist. They have a swollen gum or a tooth that hurts when they drink something cold. Asking patients to pick a specialty pushed the medical vocabulary onto the wrong person.

Instead, the home screen starts with symptoms. Each symptom maps to one or more specialties in the admin master data. The app collects every matching specialty, shows each doctor once, and lets the patient narrow by clinic, availability or specialty on top. If nothing matches, it falls back to direct search rather than a dead end.

Everything remained searchable; most patients never needed to know the word for it.

Fig 3. From symptoms to recommended doctors to a booked appointment.

Issue #4

Doctors document between two patients so I designed a session workspace that saves itself.

A dentist has minutes, not a quiet desk. Notes written in a general text field were lost to interruptions, and X-rays ended up in phone galleries with no link to the patient.

The session workspace opens with a timer and the patient pre-brief already loaded. Notes autosave every 30 seconds and lock to the appointment. Uploaded X-rays and photos are auto-tagged with date, treatment stage and patient. Templates cover the repetitive text. One action, Mark Stage Complete, closes the session and updates the queue, the record and the patient’s app at once.

This reduced documentation to what only the doctor can write.

Results

Outcomes

One record, from booking to follow-up

The impact is visible in how the product is structured rather than in a single metric:

  • four products ship from one design system, in Arabic and English
  • every appointment carries the same status for the clinic, the doctor and the patient
  • treatment plans and follow-ups keep patients attached after the first visit
  • country-scoped roles let Egypt, Canada and the USA run on one platform
