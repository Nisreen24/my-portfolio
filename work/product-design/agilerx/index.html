The challenge

Clinical work at counter speed.

Ontario pharmacists deliver a growing list of clinical services, from medication reviews and minor-ailment prescribing to vaccinations and injections, while running a busy counter. AgileRx is a companion, not another pharmacy-management system. It runs beside the dispensing software already in place and helps the pharmacist find, deliver and document billable services.

The design challenge was to reduce friction and make the right information and the next action immediately visible. Clinical work needs to be fast, clear and safe, especially when the pharmacist is working while standing, switching between patients and making time-sensitive decisions.

Product direction

Structure

Designing around the patient in front of you.

The earlier structure was organised by module: open MedsCheck, then find a patient. A pharmacist thinks the other way round. AgileRx is moving toward a patient-first structure, where one patient entry point exposes every service that applies to that patient.

The result is a simpler mental model that every clinical workflow can share.

Mobile-first principles

Rules the screens are held to.

  1. 01

    One clear action per screen

    Each screen answers one dominant question and ends in one primary button. Secondary options step back.

  2. 02

    Reduce typing and taps

    Toggles, checkboxes, segmented controls and predefined choices replace free text wherever a pick is possible. Dictation is offered where notes are unavoidable.

  3. 03

    Keep patient context visible

    Name, age and coverage stay in the session header, so the pharmacist never wonders whose record they are in.

  4. 04

    Make progress clear

    Step tabs and a progress bar show what is done, what is next and how much is left. Progress is saved if the pharmacist is interrupted.

  5. 05

    Prioritise safety-critical information

    Allergies, red flags and high-severity interactions surface first and are never nested behind a tap.

  6. 06

    Design for touch first

    Tap targets are at least 44×44px, primary actions sit in thumb reach, and nothing depends on hover.

Selected experience

01 · Home

Triage, not a dashboard.

Home is designed around four ideas: Needs Attention, Ready to Act, Eligible patients and Follow-ups. A single urgent card, one light context line and a short list of patients the pharmacist can act on right now.

UX decision

Stat grids and activity feeds were removed from Home. Everything that is not an action today sits one tap away, so the first two seconds answer "what needs me now?"

02 · Patients

One list that prioritises itself.

Search by name, health card or phone. Filter by Eligible today or Needs action. Each row shows only what is needed to recognise and prioritise a patient: name, age, the top opportunity and its fee.

UX decision

Rows are capped at two lines and one dominant status. Anything deeper lives in the patient screen, which keeps the list scannable as it grows.

03 · Patient & services

Identity, safety, then action.

The patient screen follows a fixed order: confirm identity, surface safety, then show what you can do today. Eligible services appear with their fee and a specific action, so the value of a service is part of the decision rather than hidden behind it.

UX decision

Only the top recommendation gets a solid call to action. Further services are quiet rows, so the screen still has one primary move.

04 · Clinical session

One medication per screen.

The MedsCheck worksheet reviews nine medications as nine short screens rather than one long form. Indication is pre-filled from the record, adherence is a two-option control and side effects are added only when needed.

UX decision

Micro-steps trade scrolling for progress. The pharmacist always knows they are on medication three of nine, and an interruption costs one screen, not the whole review.

Session pattern

Shared clinical session

Learn the shell once. Reuse it for every service.

MedsCheck, Minor Ailments, Vaccinations and Injections are moving onto one session structure: step-based navigation, a persistent patient context line, structured clinical inputs, an AI-assisted draft, pharmacist review, then completion. The content changes per service. The shell does not.

AI in the workflow

AI assists the pharmacist.
It does not replace clinical judgment.

Drafted rationale, suggested drug-therapy problems and plain-language notes speed up documentation. They are proposals, and the product treats them that way.

  • Always visually distinguishable, with its own violet marker used for nothing else.
  • Always editable in one tap.
  • Always reviewed by the pharmacist before it is logged.
  • Never submitted automatically.
Visual direction

Visual system

Soft clinical intelligence.

A lavender-led system with rounded forms, restrained gradients and light glass, built as white-label-friendly tokens so the same product can wear a pharmacy's own colour. Dimensional icons mark the branded service tiles. Simple line icons carry product actions. The brand essence is modern, trusted, caring and efficient.

Real clinical use

Small decisions that hold up at the counter.

  • Touch targetsEvery control is at least 44×44px with spacing that prevents mis-taps between adjacent actions.
  • Explicit labels"Complete MedsCheck", "Screening clear — continue", "Accept & log". Never "Submit" or "New assessment".
  • Lists that growSearch, filters and sort exist from day one, with incremental loading for long logs.
  • All the statesEmpty, loading and error states are designed alongside the happy path, with a plain-language recovery step.
  • Money stays legibleThe fee sits next to the service and the primary action, in tabular figures, on every actionable row.
  • Safety firstAllergies, red flags and high-severity interactions are the first visible item whenever they apply, tiered red, amber and neutral.
  • No hoverNothing depends on a pointer. Every interaction works with a single tap and a visible focus state.

How I worked

Workflow

More than static UI.

The work ran from product structure to developer handoff: shaping how the app is organised, redesigning it mobile-first, setting the token system, building the screens as an HTML/CSS prototype that developers can read directly, reviewing implementation and collaborating through Git and GitHub.

  • Developer handoff
Active development

Still evolving.

AgileRx is in active development. The mobile experience keeps evolving as product flows, clinical patterns and implementation are refined, and the move to the new mobile-first system is still under way. This case study presents selected work and design direction rather than a final product.
